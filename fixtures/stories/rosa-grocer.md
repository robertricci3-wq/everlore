Allora — okay. When I came to America I was seven years old. Same age as you, Sofia! We came on a big ship from Naples, my mother and my brother Carlo and me, to be with my father. He was already in Pittsburgh, working in the steel mill. This was 1961.

I didn't know one word of English. Not one. At school the other kids talked so fast and I just sat there. I felt like — like I was behind a glass window. Everybody could see me but nobody could hear me.

Every day after school my mother sent me to the little grocery on our street, Mr. Kowalski's. He was Polish, a big man with a white apron and a pencil behind his ear. The first day I just pointed. I pointed at the bread, I pointed at the milk. And he didn't laugh. He picked up an apple and he said, "Apple." And he waited. And I said, "Ap-pull." And he clapped! Like I did something wonderful.

So every day, one word. Apple. Bread. Milk. Onion — onion was hard. Tomato he said funny, and I said it the Italian way, pomodoro, and he tried to say pomodoro, and we both laughed so hard. By Christmas I could say the whole list myself.

When I was grown I asked him once why he took so much time with a little girl buying bread. And he said, "Somebody did it for me." He came over from Poland when he was a boy, you see. Same glass window.

So that's what I want you to know, cara. When somebody is new, and they're behind the glass, you be the one who picks up the apple. It costs nothing. Nothing. And they remember it their whole life. Look at me — I'm eighty-two and I remember.
