Well, okay. So the spring I was nine — that would've been, oh, 1956, I think, '56 — the river came up. The Cedar River. We had the farm about two miles outside of Vinton, Iowa, and most years the river just kind of minded its own business, you know.

But that spring it rained for, I want to say, eight days straight. And my dad comes in the house one night, he's soaked, water just pouring off his hat, and he says, "Walt, get your boots. The low pasture's going under."

Now we had six calves down in the low barn. Brand new, some of 'em only a couple days old. And a calf that little can't swim, not in that kind of water. So it's dark, it's pouring, and me and Dad and my dog Pepper — she was a black and white collie mix, smartest dog I ever had — we go down there with a lantern.

The water's already up over my knees in the barn. Dad's carrying the calves two at a time, one under each arm, up to the hayloft, and I'm holding the lantern and I'm scared to death, honestly. And Pepper is going back and forth, back and forth, keeping the ones that aren't carried yet from wandering off into the water. Nobody taught her that. She just knew.

The last calf, the littlest one, Dad couldn't get to it, he had two already. And he looks at me. And I'm nine years old and that calf is maybe as heavy as I am, but I got my arms around her and I, I just went. Up the ramp. Slipping the whole way. And I made it.

We sat up in that hayloft till the sun came up, all of us, calves and all, listening to the rain. And my dad put his hand on my shoulder and said, "You don't have to be big to be useful, son." I never forgot that. I tell you kids that all the time, don't I? You don't have to be big to be useful.

Um, the water went down after a couple days. We lost some fence and a lot of hay, but we didn't lose one calf. Not one.
